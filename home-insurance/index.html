Home insurance, explained properly

Home insurance protects the structure you live in, the things you own, or both. Here’s how it works on each side of the Atlantic, what’s usually covered and excluded, and how to compare policies on more than price.

What a home policy usually covers

A typical combined buildings and contents policy, before optional extras.

Usually covered

  • Fire, smoke, explosion, lightning
  • Storm damage (UK) / windstorm and hail (US, check hurricane deductibles)
  • Escape of water from pipes, tanks and appliances (the leak’s damage, not usually the leak repair)
  • Theft and attempted theft
  • Subsidence, heave and landslip (UK buildings cover, with a higher excess)
  • Falling trees, aerials and impact by vehicles or animals
  • Liability to others: property owner’s liability (buildings) and occupier’s liability (contents)
  • Alternative accommodation if your home becomes uninhabitable after an insured event

Usually not covered

  • Wear and tear, gradual deterioration, rot, damp and mould
  • Poor workmanship or faulty design
  • Damage while the home is unoccupied beyond the policy limit (often 30–60 days)
  • Flood and earthquake on most standard US homeowners policies
  • Pests and vermin
  • Theft without forced entry from a let or shared home (depends on wording)

Common add-ons

  • Accidental damage (spills, DIY mishaps, a foot through the ceiling)
  • Personal possessions cover away from the home
  • Home emergency (boiler, plumbing, locks, pests)
  • Family legal expenses
  • High-value item or scheduled-item cover (jewellery, art, bikes)
  • Water backup / sewer cover (US)

General guide only. Cover varies by insurer and policy: the policy wording and key facts document decide what you’re actually insured for.

How to compare home insurance

  1. Work out the right sums

    Buildings: rebuild cost, not market value. Contents: the cost of replacing everything as new. Room-by-room totals beat a guess.

  2. Pick the cover type

    Buildings, contents or combined (UK); HO-3, HO-5, HO-4 or HO-6 (US). Match it to whether you own, rent or let.

  3. Get like-for-like quotes

    Use the same sums, excess and extras on every quote. Check direct insurers that aren’t on comparison sites too.

  4. Read the key facts

    The key facts or IPID summary lists the main exclusions and limits. Check single-item limits and unoccupancy rules.

  5. Answer questions carefully

    Inaccurate answers can reduce or void a claim. Tell your insurer about changes such as building work or lodgers.

Buildings, contents or combined?

Buildings insurance covers the structure: walls, roof, floors, windows, and permanent fixtures such as fitted kitchens, bathrooms and (usually) outbuildings, fences and drives. Contents insurance covers what you’d take with you if you moved: furniture, clothes, electronics, jewellery and other belongings.

If you own a house you’ll normally want both, and a combined policy is often simpler (one excess structure, one claims line). If you rent, you only need contents; your landlord insures the building. Flat owners in the UK should check whether the freeholder or management company already insures the building through a block policy, which is common for leasehold flats.

How home insurance works in the US

US homeowners insurance is sold as standard policy forms that bundle dwelling, other structures, personal property, loss of use and personal liability cover:

FormWho it’s forHow it covers
HO-3Most homeownersDwelling on an “open perils” basis (everything not excluded); belongings on a named-perils basis
HO-5Newer or higher-value homesOpen perils for both the dwelling and belongings
HO-4RentersBelongings, liability and loss of use
HO-6Condo ownersInterior walls, improvements and belongings; the condo association covers the shell
DP-3LandlordsDwelling cover for a rented-out home (see landlord insurance)

Flood is excluded from standard homeowners policies, and earthquake usually is too. Flood cover comes from the National Flood Insurance Program or private flood insurers (see flood insurance). In hurricane-prone states, wind damage can carry a separate percentage-based deductible.

How much cover do you need?

  • Buildings (UK): insure the rebuild cost, which is what it would cost to clear the site and rebuild, including fees. It can be higher or lower than the market value. The figure is often in your mortgage valuation report, and the BCIS publishes a rebuild cost calculator for UK homes.
  • Dwelling (US): insure the replacement cost of the home, not its sale price (which includes land). Ask whether the policy offers extended or guaranteed replacement cost if building costs jump after a disaster.
  • Contents: add up what it would cost to replace everything new. People often underestimate this by forgetting clothes, kitchenware and garden items.

Under-insurance matters. Many policies apply an “average” clause: if you insure for less than the real value, the insurer can reduce a claim in proportion. Read our guide to under-insurance and the average clause.

Your rights when renewing (UK)

Since 1 January 2022, FCA rules stop UK home and motor insurers charging renewing customers more than they would charge an equivalent new customer through the same sales channel (the end of “price walking”). Shopping around can still save money, because a different insurer may price your risk differently. See how the renewal pricing rules work.

Fair ways to pay less

  • Compare at every renewal, including direct insurers that aren’t on comparison sites.
  • Pay annually if you can: paying monthly usually involves credit, which can add cost. See monthly vs annual payments.
  • Choose a higher voluntary excess only if you could comfortably pay it.
  • Improve security: approved locks and a monitored alarm can matter to some insurers.
  • Don’t over-insure, but never cut sums below the real value to save money.
  • Remove extras you don’t need, such as legal expenses you already have through another policy.

Frequently asked questions

Is home insurance a legal requirement?
No. In the UK, US and EU there’s no general legal duty to insure your home. But mortgage lenders almost always require buildings (dwelling) cover as a condition of the loan, and some leases and tenancy agreements require insurance too.
Should I insure my home for its market value?
No. Buildings cover should reflect the cost of rebuilding, not the price the home would sell for, which includes the land. Contents should reflect the cost of replacing items new.
Does home insurance cover flooding?
Most standard UK buildings and contents policies include flood. Most standard US homeowners policies exclude it, so you need separate flood insurance. Read our flood insurance guide.
What is an excess or deductible?
It’s the part of each claim you pay yourself. UK policies call it an excess (compulsory plus any voluntary amount); US policies call it a deductible. Some perils, such as subsidence or escape of water, can carry a higher excess.
