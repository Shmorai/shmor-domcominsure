Renters vs landlord insurance: who covers what when you rent a home

If you rent, it is easy to assume the landlord's insurance looks after everything in the property. It does not. A landlord's policy and a tenant's policy protect different things, and mixing them up is a common reason claims go unpaid.

Landlord insurance covers the building, not your things

The Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) explains that a landlord's insurance covers the building: it will pay to repair the structure, not to replace a tenant's possessions. Landlord policies usually add liability cover if a tenant or guest is injured on the property, and cover for items the owner leaves in the home, such as appliances or a lawnmower.

Many landlord policies can also include loss of rental income after an insured event. Check the wording, because that is an optional extra with some insurers. Our landlord insurance guide and buildings insurance guide explain what an owner's cover normally includes.

What renters insurance covers

Triple-I describes renters insurance as covering three things: your personal possessions (for example after theft or fire), your liability if you or your family cause injury or damage to others, and additional living expenses, such as a hotel, if the home becomes unliveable after an insured event. It does not cover the building itself.

In the UK and much of Europe the same basic split applies: the owner insures the structure, and the tenant arranges contents cover. Policies differ by country and insurer, so read the policy wording, and read your tenancy agreement, which may say who must insure what. See our renters insurance guide and contents insurance guide.

Side-by-side: who is protected for what

  • The walls, roof and fixtures: the landlord's building or landlord policy.
  • Landlord-supplied furniture and appliances: usually the landlord's policy, if the landlord has chosen to include them.
  • Your clothes, electronics and furniture: your own renters or contents policy.
  • Injury to a guest caused by your actions: your own liability cover.
  • Injury caused by a defect the landlord should have fixed: the landlord's liability cover, depending on the facts.
  • Somewhere to stay while repairs are done: additional living expenses on your policy, if included.

This is general information, not advice on your own tenancy. Where responsibility is unclear, ask the landlord or agent in writing and ask the insurer before you rely on any cover.

Replacement cost or actual cash value?

Triple-I notes that actual cash value policies deduct depreciation from a claim, while replacement cost policies pay to replace items at current prices. Replacement cost usually costs more, but Triple-I says it can be well worth the extra expense, so compare both when you choose a policy. List your belongings with rough values to avoid being under-insured: our guide to under-insurance and the average clause explains what happens if the sum insured is too low.

Roommates, landlords' requirements and what to do next

Each adult sharing a home usually needs their own cover, because one person's policy typically protects only that person's belongings unless the policy says otherwise. Triple-I also notes that many landlords require tenants to hold renters insurance before signing a lease. Before you buy, check what your policy excludes: our guide to what home insurance doesn't cover is a good checklist, and how to compare insurance shows what to look at besides price.

Frequently asked questions

Does my landlord's insurance cover my belongings?
Normally no. A landlord's policy covers the building and any items the landlord provides, not a tenant's personal property. You need your own renters or contents insurance.
Do I legally have to buy renters insurance?
Not generally by law, but some landlords make it a condition of the lease, as Triple-I notes. Check your tenancy agreement.
Does renters insurance cover the building I rent?
No. Renters insurance covers your possessions, your liability and extra living costs, not the structure, which is the landlord's responsibility to insure.
